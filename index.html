3D understanding · Submitted to ICLR 2027

GLANCE: Free-Form 3D Object Identification through Active Geometric Inspection

Identifying unfamiliar 3D objects through active visual reasoning, without predefined categories or task-specific 3D training.

Recognizing objects from geometry alone

What can an AI system recognize when an object has no color, texture, physical scale, standard orientation, or surrounding scene? GLANCE studies free-form 3D object identification: inferring an object’s identity directly from its geometry, without receiving a list of possible categories. This setting captures a central challenge in 3D understanding: a single view can be ambiguous, and the same shape can admit several reasonable descriptions.

Active inspection and hypothesis testing

GLANCE uses an off-the-shelf multimodal language model to inspect rendered views of a 3D object. It maintains competing hypotheses, predicts which geometric features would distinguish them, and selects additional viewpoints to test those predictions. Recognition becomes an iterative process of gathering evidence and resolving uncertainty.

  • Free-form recognition: Generates object identities without a predefined category vocabulary at inference time or specialized 3D training.
  • Evidence-driven viewpoint selection: Chooses new observations to resolve ambiguity between plausible identities.
  • Human-annotated benchmark: Approximately 1,000 3D shapes, each independently assigned a free-form identity and confidence score by three human annotators.
  • Semantic evaluation: Distinguishes equivalent identities, broader descriptions, related objects, and incorrect predictions to assess agreement beyond exact text matching.

Results on a controlled 100-object comparison

A single observation achieves 52% primary accuracy, while random multiview recognition reaches 73%. GLANCE reaches 75% primary accuracy with 15% fewer observations than random multiview recognition, and improves near-match accuracy from 74% to 81%. Viewpoint selection without explicit hypothesis formulation and verification reaches only 69%.

Research significance

The work connects multimodal reasoning with active perception and geometric understanding. It investigates how a model can decide what to inspect next, and how to evaluate recognition when geometry alone does not determine a single unambiguous label.

Research collaboration with Nafiseh Izadyar and my supervisor, Teseo Schneider.

Differentiable Simulation · Machine Learning · Ongoing Research

Connecting Differentiable Simulation and Machine Learning

Developing a Python and PyTorch interface to PolyFEM for simulation-based learning, sensitivity analysis, and design optimization.

From simulation to learning

This project connects PolyFEM’s finite element solver and adjoint gradients to Python-based learning workflows. It brings together programmatic simulation setup, structured outputs, and PyTorch-compatible differentiation to support data generation and gradient-based optimization.

Learning physical responses and sensitivities

Current experiments study a falling block impacting a deformable lattice, varying material stiffness, lattice thickness, and orientation. Using simulation outputs and adjoint gradients, we investigate how surrogate models can predict stress responses and their sensitivity to material changes.

  • Simulation workflows: Build parameterized experiments and extract stress responses for training and evaluation.
  • Differentiable optimization: Connect C++ forward and adjoint routines to PyTorch to explore material and geometric design updates.
  • Surrogate learning: Compare models for peak stress and stress-based objective prediction.
  • Gradient learning: Investigate a multi-head neural network that predicts objective values, material-gradient magnitudes, and signs, comparing direct gradient supervision with derivatives of a value-only model.

The broader goal is to make high-fidelity simulation easier to use in machine learning and to study how learned models can support design exploration.

Preliminary results

Current experiments achieve:

  • Peak stress prediction: 74.1% of predictions fall within 10% of simulated peak stress on the held-out test set.
  • Stress objective prediction: 97.3% of predictions fall within 10% of the simulated objective value under five-fold cross-validation.
  • Material-gradient prediction: The multi-head model achieves 97.4% gradient-sign agreement, compared with 86.9% for a value-only model differentiated through autograd. It also predicts gradient values within 20% relative error in 73.8% of cases.

These results are preliminary. Manuscript in preparation; experiments and evaluation are ongoing.

PROJECT 03

Pop Ten!

A number puzzle game with drag-to-merge controls, daily challenges, and real-time two-player matches.

React Native · TypeScript · Reanimated · Supabase

Pop Ten! turns a simple rule—merge neighboring numbers to make 10—into a game of board management and combo building. Players clear tiles, trigger special effects, and plan their next moves as the board refills.

  • Core gameplay: Drag-based merging, exact-ten clears, gravity and refill logic, combo scoring, and special Star and Bomb tiles.
  • Interactive feedback: Tile animations, clear effects, and haptic feedback make merges and special actions easier to follow.
  • Daily challenges: A shared daily setup gives players the same starting conditions and tools.
  • Real-time matches: Two players compete on separate boards with a shared starting seed and tool configuration. Timed matches include score-based results and rematch support.

Development work spans game-state logic, gesture interaction, animation sequencing, and multiplayer match flow, with iOS builds distributed through TestFlight.
